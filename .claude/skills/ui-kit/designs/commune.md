# commune

Fifteen ways one public body is drawn, nine as the notice board and six as the register. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is which question the first screen answers — whether the counter is open, what is on this week, or who decides — and how the formalities are read: as one list with the counter held beside it, as tiles on a board, or down a long page somebody studies before taking a morning off.

## Maison de commune

The village commune as most residents meet it: the day's notice beside this week's counter hours and the door on the first screen, the formalities as one list with the counter held beside it, the agenda in dated rows. For a commune of two or three thousand where nine visits out of ten ask whether the counter is open and what to bring.

## Pilier public

The pillar on the square as a page: a few words set large, then everything the commune has to say as tiles on one board — the hours, the door, the formalities, the dates, the five people who decide. For a hamlet commune of a few hundred with a counter two mornings a week, where nothing deserves a long page and the whole site is read standing up, on a phone, at the door.

A town's administration with several counters and a visitor who has never been to any of them: the town hall beside the opening words, the week across the page as a strip, the streets around the door as a panorama, then the formalities as ruled rows read down and the questions in two columns. For a town of ten or fifteen thousand whose residents study the page once, the evening before, so as to come with the right papers.

A commune known by what happens in it: the home page is a board with the next dates among its tiles, and the agenda is the long page, each date a card worth a look. For a village with a market, a fête and twenty societies, whose visitors come from the next valley as often as from the next street. Marigold is only fair on civic and chosen anyway, because this page should look like the poster for Saturday rather than like a counter.

## Commune de montagne

A commune spread along a valley, where the town hall is a drive away and closed more often than open: a photograph of the village behind the name, then the hours and the roads around the door across the page. The formalities are tiles on a board because there are few of them, and the season's dates run down a rule, from the first snow clearing to the day the herds go up. For a mountain commune whose residents and second-home owners both need the way in before anything else.

## Service de piquet

A public utility rather than a town hall: water, power and waste, looked up at night when a tap has run dry. The words alone, set large, beside the counter's hours and the depot on the map, on a dark page that does not glare at two in the morning; what the service does is a board of tiles, and the planned cuts and collections are a long page read in order. For a town's industrial services or an intercommunal water board, whose visitor wants the on-call number before the mission statement.

A commune made last year out of five villages, which has to introduce itself: the new name beside a photograph of the new town hall, the door on a wide map because half the residents have never been to it, the formalities as tiles for the few things that changed, and the first year's dates told in one column. Ledger for the measure a new authority wants, in harbour's blue rather than paper and ink, so that it reads as a public service and not as a notary. For a merged commune, an intercommunal association or a new school district.

## Bourgeoisie

A burgher corporation: an old house with forests, a cellar and a seal, seen by few people who all come on purpose. The first screen is one photograph behind the name on a dark page, the story of the house under it in one column and the door a plain map; its few dates of the year are cards — the assembly, the wood auction, the wine sale — and its council a list of names with their charges. Ember is only fair on ledger and right here: wax on dark paper for an institution four centuries old that wants to look it.

## Contrôle des habitants

The office every newcomer passes through in their first week: arrivals and departures, residence permits, the dog tax. Its visitors have just moved, often from another country, and read the page in a second language, so the first screen sets the opening words beside this week's counter hours and the door, the formalities are ruled rows read down with every answer already open, and the staff of each counter a list with what each one handles. Denim is good on civic and reads as an office rather than a village notice: for the residents' registry of a town of twenty thousand where a fifth of the people change every few years.

## Service jeunesse

The commune's youth and leisure service: holiday camps, the youth centre, the pool's season, the after-school club. The home page is a board whose tiles are the next registration deadline, the office's hours and the door; the activities are tiles read at a glance, the summer's dates cards a child can point at, and the open posts — monitors, lifeguards, the snack bar — cards as well, because most of them go to students. Sorbet is good on civic and makes a public service look like the poster on the school's door, for parents who register three children between two meetings and teenagers looking for a summer job.

## Grand Conseil

A parliament rather than an office: a city council or a cantonal Grand Council, whose site publishes the sittings, the motions and the members who vote them. The first screen is the opening words beside the gallery's opening hours and the door of the chamber, the session's sittings run down a rule told in one column like minutes, and the members are a long list of names with their group and their district. Denim is only fair on ledger and right here, the blue of a parliament's binders: for journalists, students and the citizen who wants to know when the vote is and how to sit in the gallery.
