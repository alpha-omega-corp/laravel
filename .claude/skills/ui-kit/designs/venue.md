# venue

Fourteen ways one place for hire is drawn, by what kind of place it is and how its hosts choose it: the estate a couple compares for weeks and the room above a restaurant looked up from the street, the hall that sells on its price and the building that sells on its architect, the old house with a story and the one that opened last year, the auditorium an agency books for its delegates and the barge hired for one evening on the water. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all fourteen; what changes is which fact the first screen leads with (the photograph, the numbers, the story or the door) and how the rooms are read: as a list, as plates on a wall, or beside the way in.

## Domaine des noces

A country estate that lives on weddings, chosen by couples who visit a dozen places and compare them for weeks: the first screen is the barn photographed full-bleed with the name low on it, then three doors to the rooms, the gallery and the questions. The rooms page opens straight on the list with the great barn drawn first and large, the gallery is a mosaic to linger on, the questions read down one column with every answer showing, and the request stands beside its invitation with the roads to the gate across the page under it. Vellum's black, white and one red keep the chrome out of the photographs' way.

## Orangerie au jardin

A glasshouse in a garden, hired from May to September by people who chose it on one photograph: the cover is that photograph with the name centred over it and nothing else to scroll to. After it everything reads like a magazine piece about the place: the gallery a row of pictures that pauses the story, the questions and the request in one narrow column, the form drawn on the page like a letter rather than in a box. Blossom's soft pink and violet are for a venue that is all flowers and daylight, and the name sits centred in the header and the footer as on an invitation.

## Salle du village

A communal or parish hall that sells on price and on numbers, to families and societies who ask three halls for a figure and take the cheapest that fits: the first screen is words alone, the name, how many it seats and the one action, with no photograph to wait for. The rooms are rows read down one column of capacities and prices, the questions stand open because the house rules are the contract, and the form is plain on the page. Sandstone's printed paper keeps it warm without dressing it up, and the footer is three lines.

## Séminaire au vert

A lakeside centre where firms take a team away for two days, one house of a group, booked by an assistant or an HR lead who compares capacities on a weekday morning and has to justify the choice: the first screen sets the words beside the photograph, and the rooms page is the list first. The gallery is one case told at length with the pictures as a strip, the questions run in two columns for a reader who scans, and the request stands beside its invitation over a wide map, since sixty people arrive by car. Lagoon's teal water says a day out of the office.

## Salle de spectacle

A theatre or a former cinema hired for galas, premieres and prize-givings: a house people know with the lights down, so the page is pinned dark and nocturne's violet night does what the auditorium does. Home tells the house's story before it sells anything, the rooms page leads with the main hall large and the foyers after it, the gallery is a mosaic, and the request is a quiet column with the map plain under it. For a venue with a past in town, whose hosts already have a memory of an evening there; the name stands in the middle of the header like the title on a programme.

## Grange du soir

A barn in the fields hired for the evening, the dinner and then the dancing, by hosts who want to see it lit: the cover is one photograph of the room at night and the name, in ember's oxblood and gold. The rooms page sets each room across the column, its picture beside its numbers, the enquiry opens with the form on one side and the road to the yard on the other, because a barn down a farm track is found by its map, and the questions run in two columns: caterers, music, curfew and parking are what every host of a party asks.

## Pavillon

A venue that is architecture first, a pavilion by the water or a hall restored by a known architect, hired by people who chose the building before the date: one plate and the name on a white wall, pinned light because the wall is the point. The rooms are plates on a board with their capacities as labels, the gallery a second board of uncropped pictures, and the questions and the request hang from hairlines in one column. Nothing is rearranged: every component is drawn the way the direction draws it.

## Halle industrielle

A converted factory hall opened last year and hired by agencies and brands for launches, fairs and staff parties: the newcomer, with no history to tell and a strong look to show. The photograph is bled behind the words and the page pinned dark in concrete's greys, a fair palette for most venues and the right one for a building that is concrete, and the hall itself leads the board of rooms as the large plate. The gallery is a mosaic, the questions two columns for a production manager who scans, and the request stands beside its invitation over a wide map for the lorries.

## Château classé

A listed château or abbey whose owners let its rooms to pay for the roof: the building's history is the reason to come, so home is that history told at length with plates between the paragraphs, on quill's bone paper and blue-black ink. The rooms are an index in rows (name, capacity, surface, price), the gallery a strip of pictures inside the story, the questions open in one column, and the request plain on the page with the map and the way in under it. For the old house studied for a week by a couple, a notary's office or a foundation.

## Centre de congrès

A conference centre beside the station: a plenary hall of six hundred seats, twelve rooms for workshops, a foyer for the coffee and the stands, chosen by an event agency or a federation's secretary on numbers, transport and the price of a day per delegate. The first screen sets the building beside its line, and under it a wall of short words from the organisers who came back. Cool blue-grey with hairline rules on white walls, the plates of the rooms the only loud thing. The spaces read as rows, each photograph beside its seats, its surface and its equipment, because a planner compares them line by line; the gallery is a board of plates, the hall set for a plenary, a dinner and a fair; the questions are many and sit in two columns; and the request stands beside its invitation, with a panorama of the trains, the tram and the car parks under it. The pages sit left of the name and the action right of it.

## Péniche

A barge moored on the river in town, hired for a birthday, a rehearsal dinner or a company's summer evening: a saloon for sixty, a deck for a hundred standing, and the water lit under it. It is chosen for the evening it promises, so the home opens on one photograph of the deck at dusk and reads like a story of the boat, closing on one host's words carried across the column. Dark by design, aubergine and antique gold, a little theatrical. The spaces, the saloon and the deck, stand in rows beside how to come aboard; the pictures are a magazine with one band of the river; the questions on moorings, music after eleven and the weather read down a long page with every answer open; and the request stands beside its invitation, with a panorama of the quay under it.
