# trades

Twelve ways one trade is drawn, seven at the counter and five in the studio. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is why the phone rings (a leak tonight, a bathroom next spring, a roof studied for a week) and so what the first screen leads with: the number and the area served, the price of a call-out, a family's name, or the finished work photographed large. They differ too by how the jobs are shown (tiles on a board, a long page, a magazine), by whether the questions are a list to scan or a story to read, and by whether the quote form stands alone or beside its invitation.

## Artisan du quartier

The plumber, electrician or joiner most people mean when they say "ours": a small firm in a small town, called for a leak this week and a bathroom next year. The first screen holds the name beside a photograph of finished work, with the three facts a caller wants under it (since when, how far, what a call-out costs) and the workshop on the map beside them as the middle of the area served. The jobs are tiles on one board, the questions a list scanned from the top of a short page, and the quote form stands plain on its own. Warm paper and a serif, considered and unhurried: nothing here sells, it answers.

## Urgence de nuit

The plumber or locksmith on call, found on a phone at the door at eleven at night with water on the floor. No photograph is owed: the opening is the words alone, set large, yellow on black like the lettering on the van, and the dark is pinned because that is when the page is read. The streets served run under it as a panorama; the call-out charge is on the questions page with every answer already open; and the jobs are one row of pictures that scrolls, because nobody in a hurry came for them. The footer is three lines.

## Entreprise familiale

A family firm in its second or third generation, known in the district by its surname: the name is centred over the pages like the board over the workshop door, and the opening is centred too, over a wide photograph of the team in front of the vans, with the workshop beside it. Deep green on warm paper with drawn borders, sturdy and a little old-fashioned on purpose. Its visitors already know the name and come to check it is still the same house, so the work is a long page of jobs all the same size, thirty years of them and none louder than another, the questions are set in two columns, and the quote page puts the invitation beside the form.

## Grands travaux

The roofer, the façade firm or the heating contractor with three teams, whose job costs as much as a car and is studied for a week before anybody calls. The photograph lies behind the words across the whole opening, a roof or a scaffolded front seen whole, and every page but the last is long: the work as a mosaic where every fifth site is shown twice the size, the questions in two columns because there are many (permits, subsidies, how long, who is insured). Cool blue-grey with hairline rules, the look of an offer on letterhead; only the quote page is short, the form alone under its heading.

## Prix affichés

The one that sells on price: a fixed call-out, an hourly rate and a free quote, said before anything else. No photograph, the words set large with the three figures under a rule and the area served on the map beside them. Indigo with stitched edges, the colour of the overalls, plain-spoken. The questions page is the tariff, so every answer stands open on a short page; the jobs are a board with the pictures as one row, proof and no more; and the quote page is the long one, the invitation beside the form and what happens next under it, because a visitor who compared prices wants to know what sending it commits to.

## Tournée des villages

The country tradesman whose round is thirty villages and whose first question on the phone is always whether he comes out that far. The long home page opens on the name centred over a wide photograph of the valley and then gives the map the whole width, because the area is the argument. Terracotta and olive on warm paper, hand-made. The jobs are a long page captioned by village, so a visitor looks for a neighbour's; the questions keep the short page, the call-out answered at the top; and the quote page is long, the form plain on the page with the story of what helps to price a job after it.

## Petits travaux

The newcomer who set up alone last year and takes the jobs the big firms decline: a tap that drips, a lamp to hang, a radiator to bleed. There is no long history to show, so the opening is the name beside a portrait of the person who will ring the bell, on peach paper in a rounded face with raspberry links: a palette that is only fair for a counter and right here, because the page must say that no job is too small and no question silly before it says anything else. The few jobs done fit one board, the questions stand open, and the form is short on a short page.

## Ébénisterie

The joiner or cabinetmaker who sells on craft: a staircase, a kitchen in oak, a door rehung in a listed house. The first screen is one full-height photograph of the work with the name low on it and the workshop along its foot, and almost nothing else. Monochrome with one red and square corners, so the wood is the only colour on the page. The work is set like a magazine, the story of a job in a narrow column and the pictures as a mosaic to look at closely; the questions are few and all open in the same column; the quote page is a poster again, the form plain under it.

## Bains sur mesure

A bathroom fitter with a showroom, chosen by people who are redoing one room and will live with it for twenty years. Teal water on a pale aqua page, calm and fresh. The long home page sets the words beside the photograph of a finished room, then what a job includes, then the showroom on a panorama of its streets, since this is the one trade people do visit. The work reads as a magazine, room by room; the questions are many (how long without water, what stays, what it costs) and set in two columns; and the quote page puts the invitation to come and see beside the form.

## Poêles et cheminées

The stove fitter and chimney builder: a purchase made once, in autumn, by somebody who has looked at pictures of fires for a month. Oxblood and gold on warm paper, candlelit. The work is a long page where every fifth fire is shown twice the size; the questions (the flue, the permit, the fine-dust rules) are told as a story in a narrow column before the list; and the quote page is long, the invitation beside the form.

## Mise en lumière

An electrician who designs lighting: gardens, façades, a kitchen whose worktop is finally lit. Light is what the firm sells and it only shows after dark, which is why the dark is pinned and why a palette that is only fair for a studio is the right one here: violet on a night sky, the panels edged by a glow. The photograph of a lit house lies behind the words of the long home page; the work is a magazine with one row of pictures that scrolls like a strip of film; the questions are answered in the same narrow column; and the quote page is a poster, the form under one sentence.

## Peintre décorateur

The decorative painter and plasterer, lime wash, stucco and gilding, whose colours no screen shows truly. So the opening owes no photograph: it is a poster of words alone, the name centred over the pages and the sentence set large in a high-contrast serif, aubergine on blush white with a little antique gold. The light is pinned because a colour is judged by day. The work is a long page of pictures all the same size, read like a colour chart, each captioned with what was applied; the questions are in two columns; and the quote page is a poster with the form plain under it.
