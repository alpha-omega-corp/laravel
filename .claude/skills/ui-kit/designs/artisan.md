# artisan

Twelve ways one maker is drawn: five hung on atelier's white walls, four set out on market's stall and three in studio's editorial calm. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is what the first screen leads with (a wall of pieces, one piece, a sentence, the stall beside the way to it), how the pieces are read (plates, rows with the price at the end, one put forward, a price list in a narrow column) and who is being answered: somebody at the market with a phone, somebody weighing a commission for a month, a cook comparing blades, a family that has always bought from this house.

## L’étagère

The maker most people picture: a potter with a workshop on a town street, whose pieces are few, priced and looked at before anything else. The home page is a wall of plates and the pieces page the same wall in full, each price beside its name. The workshop is read rather than browsed, one column on how a bowl is made with its photographs as a single row that pauses the prose; the markets and the commission are the plain long page, because those visitors already know what they came for. For an established workshop whose visitors want to see what is on the shelf this month and what it costs.

## Pièce unique

One piece and the name: the cover is a single photograph bled to the window's edges, the maker's name centred over the pages like a label sewn inside. For a luthier, a cabinetmaker or a jeweller who finishes a dozen pieces a year and whose client studies the site for a month before writing. The pieces page puts the one that is ready first, at twice the size; the making runs down one rule, because the drying takes weeks and the firing a day; the workshop's pictures are a mosaic to look at closely, and the commission is a letter, its form bare on the page. Quill's bone paper and blue-black ink are quieter than white, and suit work kept for a lifetime.

## Sur fond noir

Glass, raku, silver and blades are photographed against the dark, and so is this site: the scheme is pinned dark, so no visitor ever sees the plates on white. The first screen is one lit piece and the name, the map bare at its foot; the pieces and the workshop are then boards of plates that glow against the ground, the workshop's own pictures one row that scrolls. The few open days each get a card, since a firing night is something to come to, and the footer closes in three lines under the title block. For a glassblower or a raku potter whose every picture is taken by the light of the kiln.

## Carnet d’atelier

A workshop that sells on how things are made opens on a sentence set large, with no photograph at all, and then tells: the workshop, the season's markets and the commission are each one column of prose on warm printed paper, with the steps, the dates or the form under it. The steps and the dates both run down a rule, read in order like entries in a notebook, and the commission sets its invitation beside the form. Only the pieces are a board, because prices are looked up, not read. For a weaver, a dyer or a potter who writes a newsletter people actually read, and whose clients buy the story with the piece.

## La forge

A knife-maker, a blacksmith or a metal workshop in an industrial unit. Concrete's grey and single orange are harsher than this look asks for and are chosen anyway, because the workshop is steel, soot and a poured floor. Everything is a board, the home page, the workshop and the dates, so a visitor sees the whole business at once, the words beside the photograph on the opening; the pieces are rows, the picture beside the words, read for steel, length and price rather than admired. The steps join across the column as equals, the pages stand left of the name in the header and the footer is three lines. For a maker whose buyers are cooks, hunters and collectors who compare specifications.

## Jour de marché

The newcomer who sells from a stall. Most visitors look the site up on a phone on Saturday morning, to know whether the stall is out and where the workshop is, so the home page sets the stall beside the map and the markets page goes straight to the dates, down one rule in the order of the season. The pieces are a board of glass panels with their prices, and the commission page puts its invitation beside the form, on the half of the screen a thumb reaches. Pinned light: it is a stall in the sun. For a maker in their first years, with more market days than stock.

## Portes ouvertes

A workshop in the country that people drive out to twice a year: the first screen is a wide photograph of the yard with the words centred over it, and under the three ways in a panorama of the roads around the door, because the address alone gets nobody there. The open days and the fairs are few and each gets a card on a board; the workshop page is a board too, its pictures a mosaic with every fifth one large. Orchard's deep green and drawn borders look like the place: a barn, a kiln, a field. For a potter, a basket-maker or a woodturner whose year turns on two open weekends.

## Petits prix

The stall that sells on price: bowls at 24.–, seconds at half, everything with its figure. The home page is a board that shows the lot at once; the pieces are rows with the picture beside the words and the price at the end, read like a tariff; the making is five equal steps in one row rather than a tale, and its photographs one row that scrolls past. The markets page opens on the name and goes straight to the dates, the commission form stands bare, and the header and the footer are trimmed to lines. Marigold is loud and cheerful on purpose: a yellow awning seen from across the square. For a production potter, a soap or candle maker, anyone whose pieces leave by the dozen.

## Marché de Noël

A maker who earns the year in five weeks of December evenings: the cover is the lit stall at night, bled to the window's edges, and the scheme is pinned dark so the glass panels sit on nocturne's night sky. Nocturne is rated fair for this look, electric where a stall is warm, and it is right for this one case: garlands, mulled wine, a market after dark. The pieces page puts one gift forward at twice the size, and the dates run down a rule from the first Advent weekend to the last. For a candle-maker, a blower of glass baubles, a jeweller of small gifts.

## Maison de famille

The old house: a pottery, a cutler's or a goldsmith's in its third generation, which sells on its name and the year over the door. The name is centred over the pages, the first screen centres its words over one wide photograph of the workshop, and ember's oxblood and gold, with buttons in spaced capitals, read like the label the house has always used. The pieces are a price list in a narrow column, as a wine list is; the history and the season's fairs are told at length, the dates down one rule; and the commission is the plain long page, the invitation beside the form. For a family firm whose clients' parents were clients.

## Sur commande

A cabinetmaker, a saddler or a bespoke shoemaker for whom nearly everything is made to order: the first screen sets the ask beside a piece on the bench, and every page leads to the form. The pieces are examples with their prices, in rows read rather than browsed; the workshop page opens on the road of a commission, down one rule because the drawing takes a week and the making two months, its photographs one row that pauses it. The commission itself is a letter in one column, the form bare under the story; the map is a panorama of the streets on the home page and the footer three lines. Clay's terracotta and olive on warm paper are the bench and the hide. For a maker with a waiting list rather than a shelf.
