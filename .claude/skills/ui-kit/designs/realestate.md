# realestate

Twelve ways one estate agency is drawn, across the three looks it may take: the counter that puts this week's objects and the phone first, the atelier that hangs each property as a plate, and the studio that shows a few addresses in a lot of air. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all twelve; what changes is who the first screen is for (a tenant at the door with a phone, an owner weighing whom to trust with a sale, a buyer who studies one house for a week) and so which page gets the list, the story or the form first.

## Régie de quartier

The town agency everybody knows from its window: the name beside the street map on the first screen with three figures under it, and the stock in rows from the top of its page, the photograph beside the facts. For a régie that sells and lets in its own streets, where most visitors want to know what is new this week and where to walk in.

## Guichet des locations

Lettings before anything else: a bright counter for tenants who look on a phone between two viewings. The words stand centred over one wide photograph, the whole stock is a board seen at once, the streets around the door run across the home page, and the form is plain on its page over a footer cut to three lines. For an agency whose year is a hundred leases, where the visitor wants the flats, then the door, then the form.

## Gérance

A property manager whose client is the owner of a building, not the tenant: a sober lake blue, the pages left of the name and the action right, and what the firm does ruled as a list that is read down like a mandate. The owners' words flow in columns under it, and the valuation is a long page with the invitation beside its form. For a gérance or a group with buildings under management, where the stock is short and the services are the offer.

For a young fixed-fee agency that is compared with the old houses and wins on the figure.

## Maison de famille

What it does is a row of tiles, the people are rows with a line each, and the form sits beside its invitation on a long page. For an old family firm whose argument is that it was there before and will be there after.
