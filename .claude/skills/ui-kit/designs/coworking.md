# coworking

Sixteen ways one shared workspace is drawn, eight as a launch page, six as a quiet studio and two drawn like the booking app they run on. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is what the first screen leads with (the promise, one sentence, the room in a photograph, the door and how far it is from the station), how the plans are read (side by side, as rows on a board, as a price list in a narrow column) and how much the room is shown rather than argued: the town coworking and the newcomer, the one that sells on price and the one that sells its light, the desk taken for a day off a phone and the office a team studies for a week, the members' table and the town's incubator, the station and the village, the crèche and the silent room.

## Atelier partagé

The front page most shared workspaces end up with: one promise centred over the room, what every member gets, the three things people come for, and the same ask at the foot. For a town coworking a few years old that sells the whole range, from the day pass to the closed office, to a visitor who has two other spaces open in other tabs. The plans stand side by side on a long page with the questions under them, the room and the agenda are boards read at a glance, every fifth picture large, and the visit form sits beside its invitation with the map next to it.

## Ouverture

A space in its first season: the promise alone on a dark first screen, set large with no photograph, because the room is still being fitted and the pictures are few. For the newcomer that opens its doors next month and needs founding members more than it needs to explain itself. The plans are a short page with every answer left open, the few pictures pass as one row, the opening dates are cards on a board, each worth its own look, and the visit form is drawn on the page with nothing round it; the footer is three plain lines so the page ends on its ask.

## Tarif net

A space that wins on its price and says so: the opening is a sentence set large with no picture to pay for, what a member gets is a ruled list read down like a tariff, and the plans are a board where they lie in rows, each price at the end of its line, with the questions in two columns beside them. For the challenger to a dearer address in the centre, whose visitor counts what a desk costs per day and compares line by line. Fresh is only fair on launch, louder than a workspace usually wants, and is chosen so the cheaper offer looks like the lively one. The visit is one long page: the form on the page and the streets across it.

## Côté gare

The space people look up on a phone on the platform: the cover is one photograph of the room with the name on it, and how far the door is along its foot. For a coworking beside a station that lives on day passes and on commuters between two trains, whose visitor wants to know where it is, whether it is open and what a day costs, in that order. What a pass includes is a handful of tiles, the plans are rows on a board with the day pass first, the map stands beside the form with no panel round it, and the header sets the name apart from the one action so a thumb finds it. Harbour is good on launch: a cool blue-grey that reads like signage.

## Maison de village

The house gets its story told as a journal, the few events are cards because each is a village evening, every answer stands open, and the visit is one long page with the streets across and the form beside its invitation.
