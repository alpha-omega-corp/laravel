# hotel

Fifteen ways one small hotel is drawn, seven by lamplight and eight in daylight. They differ by who is coming and what they have to settle before they do: whether the house is old or new, in a village, in town or at the end of a lane; whether the night is sold on price, on craft or on mood; whether it is booked for one night from a phone at the door or chosen over a week at a desk; and whether the guests are two, a family or somebody travelling alone for work. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all fifteen; what changes is which of them the first screen leads with: the house, the price of a night, or the way in after dark.

## Maison de village

The family hotel most people mean by the word: nine rooms in a village house, booked for a weekend by somebody who saw one photograph of it. The cover is that photograph, the house at dusk with its name over it and the way in along its foot; the rooms are one quiet column of names and prices read like a wine list, and the pictures a plain grid on a long page. Arriving is told as a story before the questions, because the door is up a lane with no street lamp, and the stay is asked for on the page beside the map, with no panel round the form.

## Vieille maison

The old house that has taken guests since 1900 and is studied for a week before an anniversary is booked in it. The name is centred over a wide view of the façade like a postcard, in black, white and one red with square corners, and centred again over the pages like a letterhead; everything in the footer sits on one column. The rooms and the pictures are each read as a journal, the pictures one row that scrolls as a pause in the story; the questions are many and set in two columns, and the stay is asked for on the long page, the invitation on one side and the form on the other, over a map with no panel round it.

## Table et chambres

Four rooms over a restaurant: people come for the table and stay the night rather than drive home, so the first screen is the name beside the room's photograph with the door at its foot, and nothing asks to be studied. Warm paper tones and a serif on the dark, the rooms listed in the same quiet column the carte downstairs is written in. The pictures are a mosaic because the dining room is half of what is sold, the few questions everybody asks (the key after service, breakfast, the car) stand open, and the stay is asked for beside the way in. The footer is three lines.

## Nuit à deux

Two nights away for two, sold on mood rather than on price: the suite, the bath under the window, dinner on Saturday. Aubergine and antique gold; one photograph and the name hold the first screen with the way in at its foot, and the booking page is the same single screen with the form quietly under it. The rooms are told one by one in a journal before their prices are given, the pictures are a mosaic worth looking at closely, and arriving after dark is a story of its own. The pages sit left of the name and the action right of it; the footer is one centred column and the map has no panel.

## Chambres à la ferme

Three rooms on a farm at the end of a lane, an hour from anywhere: the visitor wants to know that it is worth the road, what the night includes and which road. So the home page is the long one, the house first, then the three doors and a panorama of the lanes around the gate; the rooms are few and each is a card, the farm's year is read as a journal with its pictures in one row, and the handful of questions (boots, dogs, breakfast from the farm) stand open. Terracotta and olive by lamplight, a palette that is good here rather than best and right for a house built of the same earth; the stay is asked for beside its invitation, over the same wide map.

## Pension de famille

The guest house that takes children gladly: a family room with bunks, a cot at no charge, tea at four. Soft pink and violet with round corners and no borders, a palette that is only fair by lamplight and right here, because the page has to say "bring them" before anything else. The name is centred over a wide photograph on a long home page; parents study the price list, so the rooms and what a night includes are set side by side, and their many questions in two columns. The pictures are read like a holiday album, a story and then the photographs, and the stay is asked for on the page, beside the way in.

## Étape du soir

A night on the way: the hotel by the pass road or the station, booked the same afternoon and found on a phone from the pavement after dark. No photograph is owed, so the opening is words alone, set large, with the door and the way in at the foot of the one screen; the booking page is that screen again with a plain form under it. Violet on a night sky, a palette that is good here and right for a house whose sign is lit at midnight; the pages sit left of the name and the action right of it. Nothing is a story: the rooms are a list, the pictures one row to flick through, the answers about the key and the car stand open, and the footer is three lines.

The rooms and the photographs are set like a magazine, the pictures a mosaic because they are the loud part; the practical page is the plain long one, and the stay is asked for on a single quiet screen, the form with no panel round it.

## Bord du lac

A summer house by the water, open from May to September and booked by the week in January. The light is pinned because the look is daylight on the lake: teal and coral on pale aqua, the name centred over a wide photograph, then the three doors and a panorama of the shore road. What a week costs comes first, so the rooms page opens on the price list itself with its sections side by side (the rooms, what is included, the seasons); the pictures are one row to swipe through, getting there is told as a story, and the stay is asked for beside its invitation. The footer is three lines.

## Pied-à-terre

The town hotel for people who travel for work and book on price: the same room every month, an invoice, a late key. No photograph is owed, so the opening is a statement, the words set large, with the way in at the foot of the one screen; the rooms page has no opening at all, only the price list with its dotted leaders. Cool blue-grey with hairlines and tight corners, a palette that is only fair for a studio and right for a house that sells being exact. The many practical questions are set in two columns, the form stands beside its invitation over a map with no panel, the pages sit left of the name with the action right of it, and the footer is three lines.

## Belle étoile

A lodge above the fog that is sold on its night sky: no street lamp for a mile, a telescope on the terrace, the Milky Way from the bed. The dark is pinned because the sky is the look, violet on near-black with panels edged by a faint glow, a palette that is only fair for a studio and the one that turns into a night sky. One photograph lies behind the name on the single first screen with the way up at its foot; the rooms are few and each a card, the pictures a mosaic, and the road up after dark is told as a story before the questions. The stay is asked for on the long page with a plain form, over a map with no panel.

## Maison du vigneron

Three rooms in a winemaker's house above the terraces of Lavaux, let from April to the harvest to people who came for the wine and would rather sleep among the vines than drive back down. The first screen sets the words beside one photograph of the terrace at dusk, a glass on the wall; the rooms page has no opening and leads with the offer itself, the three rooms, the tasting in the cellar and the breakfast from the family's table each a card with its price, the train times and the way down held beside it. Terracotta and olive on warm paper, a palette that is good for a studio and right for a house of the same stone as its terraces; the year at the estate is read as a journal with its pictures in one row, the questions (the last train, the cellar's hours, children) are set in two columns, and the stay is asked for beside its invitation over a panorama of the slope.

Each room is told in a journal under its artist's name before its price is given, the few questions stand open in a column of their own, and the pictures are a mosaic because every room is worth a close look. ## Hôtel de cure

An old hotel in a spa town, booked for one week or three by guests over sixty who come for the baths and the walks and read every line about half board before they telephone. The light is pinned, because these readers want dark letters on a pale page: oxblood and gold on warm paper with a high-contrast serif, a palette that is good for a studio and right for a house that has kept its brass. The words are centred over a wide photograph of the façade on a long home page; the rooms page opens on the price list itself with its sections side by side (the rooms, half board, the week's cure at the baths), the long list of practical questions is set in two columns, and the stay is asked for on one quiet screen with a plain form under it. The footer is three lines.
