# farm

Fifteen ways one farm is drawn, nine on the market's board of glass panels and six at the counter. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is which fact the first screen leads with — what is in season, the hours and the way in, one crop, a date — how the range is read, as a stall to look at, a list led by one thing or a price list in rows, and whether the season's dates are a calendar read in order or a few days that each deserve a look.

## Étal de saison

The farm most people mean: it sells what it grows in its own yard and at the Saturday market. The first screen is a short opening over a board, what is in season as the large tile and the shop's hours beside the way in under it, and the market dates run down a rule beside their heading. For a family farm whose visitors ask three things in order: what there is this week, whether the shop is open, and which track to turn into.

## Verger

A fruit farm with one crop and one season: the opening sits beside the hours and the way in, because people drive out on a Saturday in October and need the gate before anything else, and the range is led by one thing, twice the size of the rest. The harvest days, the pressing and the open orchard are cards on a board, each worth a look. For an orchard, a berry grower or a vineyard's farm shop, drawn in a deep green with borders that look inked by hand.

## Paniers du vendredi

A box scheme run by young growers, read for a week before anybody signs up: a long home page that opens on the promise, centred over a wide photograph of the garden, then the pick-up days as a strip and the lane on a panorama. The box is the first item of the range at twice the size, and the delivery dates are a calendar read in order. For a market garden that sells by subscription and has to explain itself, loud and cheerful in yellow and black.

## Marché du soir

A farm known for what happens after work: the evening market in the yard, the lanterns, the harvest supper. The photograph of the yard at dusk bleeds to the edges behind the name, the board under it and the dates page are both tiles, and every date is a card. Pinned dark, where this palette turns into a night sky: for a farm whose busiest hour is seven in the evening and whose customers look it up on the way home.

## Alpage

A mountain farm that sells its cheese at the chalet for four months a year: no photograph on the first screen, only the name set large beside the days somebody is up there and the road in, since the visitor is a walker deciding whether the climb ends at an open door. The range is a short list read more than looked at, and the season, from the climb up to the descent, runs down a rule on a long page. Teal water and a pale page, calm as a day off.

For a farm that sells on its history and its craft, in terracotta and olive with a chiselled serif.

## Moulin

A grain farm with its own mill: flour, oats, lentils and a loaf on Saturdays, sold from the mill door and at two markets. The first screen sets the opening words beside the mill, the week's opening as a strip and the track along the stream on a wide map; the range is a list read by name, flour by flour, and the market days are cards on a long page, each a town and a morning. For a farm that sells staples rather than a season, on warm printed paper the colour of its sacks: sandstone is good on the market and quieter than its fruit colours.

## Ferme des enfants

An open farm for school classes and children's birthdays, with a shop for the parents at the gate: goats to feed, eggs to collect, a tractor ride on Wednesdays. The first screen is a photograph of a child at the goat pen behind the name, then a board with the week's open afternoons as a strip and the car park on a wide map; the range — the shop's eggs and juice, the birthday packages — is led by one thing at twice the size, and the open afternoons are cards on a board, one each. Sorbet is good on the market and as bright as a drawing on the fridge, for parents who plan a Wednesday afternoon on a phone.

## Pisciculture

A trout farm in a valley, its ponds fed by a spring and its shop in the hatchery, where the fish are sold whole, filleted or smoked. The first screen is a short opening over a board, the shop's hours and the bridge over the stream among its panels; the range is a price list in rows from the top of its page, led by the smoked fillet at twice the size with the hours held beside it, and the angling days and smoking sessions run down a rule beside their heading. Lagoon is good at the counter, clear water and a pale page, for a farm whose visitors come for Friday's fish and stay for the ponds.
