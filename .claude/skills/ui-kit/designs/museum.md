# museum

Fifteen ways one museum is drawn, across the three looks it may take: work hung on white walls, a public notice board, a calm gallery. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is what the first screen leads with — one work, today's hours or the title of the show — and how each page is read: the exhibitions as plates on a wall, as a list of dates or as one show told at length, the collection hung as a grid or told room by room, the visit as the tickets first, as the hours beside the door or as a page of prose.

## Cimaises

The town's fine-arts museum as most people picture one: a single painting from the current exhibition and the name on the first screen, the hours and the door along its foot, then the exhibitions and the collection each hung as numbered plates on one wall. For a museum with a show that changes twice a year and a collection worth a second visit, whose visitor wants to see what is on the walls before deciding to come.

## Fonds permanent

An old learned house whose permanent collection is the reason to travel: the long home page says what it holds with one masterpiece set beside the words like a catalogue page, the works hang as a strict grid of numbered plates, and the exhibition and the visit are written out at length. For the museum people study for a week before the trip, and that lenders and researchers read as closely as visitors do.

## Chambre claire

A house of photography or of works on paper that runs several small shows at once: the home page is the long one, its words centred over one wide photograph, with the week across it as a strip and the streets around the door as a panorama; the shows are plates on a board with each date a card, and the collection, which is kept in drawers rather than hung, is told as a story with plates between the paragraphs. For the day-tripper who plans the train before the visit.

## Friche

An art space in a former factory with hardly a collection of its own: the title of the current show set large on a dark page, words alone, and the show itself told at length; what was shown before is one row of pictures that scrolls, a pause in the page and not a wall. Concrete is only fair on these walls, and it is chosen for that: grey, industrial, the look of the building. For the newcomer with video and installation on the programme and a public that comes for the opening night.

## Vieilles pierres

A castle, an abbey or a historic house, where the building is the exhibit: the walls themselves behind the name on the first screen, the rooms told one after another with a row of them to scroll through, and the way up there written as prose before the tickets. What is on this season — the night visits, the guided tours — is a few cards that each deserve a look. For a heritage site in the country that people drive to once, on a Sunday.

## Musée communal

The commune's own museum, the one residents look up at the door with a phone: the name beside today's hours and the entrance on the first screen, the programme as a list of dates read in order, the tickets in rows with every answer already open, and the hours held beside them again on the visit page. For a public house that owes its town two answers before any picture: is it open, and what is on.

Marigold is only fair on a notice board, and it is chosen anyway: a museum for children can afford to be the warmest thing on the screen.

## Nocturne

A space for contemporary work that opens late and lives by its evenings: a dark page, the photograph behind the name, and the agenda first on its own page as dates down a rule, with nothing to scroll past before tonight's opening. The collection is a mosaic set in a story, and the visit is a few quiet lines. Nocturne is only fair for a studio and right for this one, which is never seen by daylight. For a young public that looks it up on a phone at seven in the evening.

## Musée de l’usine

A museum of industry in the mill or the watch factory that made the town: machines kept running, a demonstration on the first Sunday of the month, visitors who are retired workers, families and engineers on a day out. Indigo on a washed page with the rust of a rivet and panels edged in stitching, a palette that is good for a notice board and right for a house of machines. The name stands beside the factory hall with today's hours and the gate on the other half, and the week runs across the page as a strip; the demonstrations and the shows are the list their page opens on, the dates down a rule, while the machines hang as a board where every fifth picture is twice the size. The visit opens on the tickets with the hours and the door held beside them, the questions (groups, what may be touched, the lift) are set in two columns, and a panorama of the old industrial quarter shows the way in. The footer is three lines.

## Musée du lac

A museum of the lake on its shore, an aquarium of the fish that live in it and the boats of the last fishermen, open every day in summer and visited by families in sandals who decide that morning because the sky is grey. The light is pinned: teal water and a coral sun on a pale aqua page, a palette that is good for a notice board and right for a house on the water. The shore lies behind the name across the opening of a long home page, the hours as plain lines under it; the summer's feeding times and workshops are each a card, the fish and the boats are the long page with one row of pictures to swipe through, and the visit is two halves, the hours and the landing stage beside the name, then the tickets and the few questions everybody asks, every answer open. The name is centred over the pages.

## Maison d’écrivain

The house where a writer lived and wrote, kept as it was: the study, the manuscripts, the garden, a reading on Thursday evenings, visited by readers who come once and slowly. Every page is read rather than scanned: oxblood and gold on warm paper, a palette that is good for a studio and right for a house of books, with the name centred over the pages like a title page. Home is the long page, the words beside the study's photograph; the season's readings run down a rule in a magazine's column, the rooms and the manuscripts are told one after another with a row of pages to turn through, and the visit is a page of prose with the hours in it, every answer open and the lane up to the gate drawn plain. The footer is one centred column.
