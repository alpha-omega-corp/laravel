# architect

Twelve ways one practice is drawn, seven hung on atelier's white walls and five in studio's editorial calm. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is what the first screen leads with (a wall of plates, one photograph, a sentence or a face), how the record is read (plates, rows beside their facts, an index without pictures, a list in a narrow column) and who is being reassured: a family, a jury, a committee or a neighbour.

## Murs blancs

The practice most people picture: fifteen years of houses, conversions and a school, hung as numbered plates on one wall from the first screen and again, in full, on the work page. The method and the studio are read rather than browsed, each one column of prose with its steps or its people under it, and the registers are ruled as rows a client can check. For an established office whose clients look for a building like theirs first and only then ask who drew it.

## Maison primée

One house and the name: the cover is the photograph that won the prize, bled to the window's edges, and nothing competes with it on the first screen. The work page then sets a few large jobs across the column, each photograph beside its facts, and the studio page is a second poster, because the two partners are the practice. For a small office known for one building, whose clients have already seen it in a magazine and come to find out whether these are the people; the form is bare on the page, as a letter would be.

## Concours

An office that lives on competitions and public mandates opens on a sentence set large, with no photograph, and tells its last won competition at length under it. The record is an index, ruled rows of place, year and rank, because a jury or a commune's building officer counts schools before looking at one; the road from brief to handover runs down one rule, its stages as unequal as a public mandate's, and the registers are a list read by number. Concrete's grey and single orange are harsher than this look asks for, and are chosen anyway: the buildings are concrete and the readers are professionals.

## Carnet de chantier

A conversion told like a site diary: the home page is one farm at length on warm printed paper, plates between the paragraphs. The work page keeps to a few large jobs in rows, the method runs down a timeline because the permit takes months and the handover a day, and the people are a list, each with what they do on site. The contact page reads as a letter, the invitation beside the form and the map bare under it. For an office that converts farms and old houses and sells on how the works are run: its clients fear the site, not the drawing.

## Bureau du bourg

The office on the village square that has drawn half the houses round it: the name beside a photograph and the telephone in sight, then long plain pages that each answer a practical question. The record is an index without photographs, long because it is thirty years of extensions, barns and permits; the steps stand in one row; the registers are a list; the form sits beside its invitation, and the map is a panorama of the streets round the door. Only the studio and the contact pages are told as prose, since the family's three generations are its best argument. For the old house a neighbour looks up on a phone to ask what an extension costs and whether the commune will allow it, in quill's bound-volume navy: credentialed and unhurried.

## En ville

A town practice that builds for cooperatives and communes: the home page is a board, because it has several things going at once, housing, a school, a square, under words centred over one wide street photograph. The work page sets a few large buildings in rows, the number of flats as the figure, and the proof is a muted row of marks, since its clients are known by their logo. Harbour's cool blue-grey is dense and professional, the tone of a tender. For an office whose client is a committee that meets twice before it writes.
