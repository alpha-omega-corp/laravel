# transport

Fourteen ways one transport firm is drawn: six as a working yard, four as a counter that gets to the point, four as a service booked from a phone. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is what is carried and who is asking (a household once in ten years, a dispatcher every week, a traveller at the kerb or on the quay, a site manager with a load nobody else will take), and so what the first screen leads with: the fleet and its figures, the price list, or one promise and one button.

## Dépôt

The regional carrier with its own depot, the firm most towns have one of: removals, pallets and a dozen lorries with the family name on the door. The first screen sets the name and its three figures beside the depot, what is carried reads down as ruled rows with a price on each, and the rates stand as slabs on one board so a household and a dispatcher each find their own. The quote page is its opening and the form drawn straight on the page, with no band after it to ask twice. For a firm of thirty that is asked for a price ten times a day and wants to look like the yard it works from.

## Quai de charge

A freight depot seen from the loading dock: what is carried as tiles on one board, the networks it belongs to as one row of marks, and yellow and black as loud as a barrier. For a pallet and groupage carrier whose visitors are dispatchers and workshop owners who already know what they need and want to see at a glance whether this depot takes it. The rates run down a long page with the questions in two columns, and the footer is three lines because the board has already said everything.

## Longue distance

The long-haul firm at night: one photograph of a lorry on the motorway behind a name as large as a hoarding, on a dark instrument-panel ground. For a group that runs forty-tonners to Zurich, Milan and Lyon, sells capacity to industry by the kilometre and has to hire drivers all year. The rates are rows with the price at the end of each, and the careers page goes from its opening straight to the posts, each one a card with its own paragraph and nothing after them but the footer, because a driver reads that page as closely as a client reads the rates.

## Transfert de bureaux

An office and workshop removals firm chosen by a facility manager who compares three bids over a week. The long page opens with the words beside a floor being cleared on a Saturday, then the services as rows and the clients and certificates as one quiet row of marks; the rates are a board to scan in a meeting, and the quote is the invitation on one side and the form on the other. Cool blue-grey and hairlines, good on yard rather than best, and chosen because it is neutral enough to sit in a tender file.

## Vieille maison

The removals house that has had its name on lorries for three generations and moves pianos, clocks and whole family homes. Black on white with one red, as its livery has always been: the palette is only fair on yard and is chosen for exactly that, pinned light so the page stays paper. The cover is the name alone, set very large over its three figures; the licences are read as a list with their years, and the header and the footer are centred like a letterhead. For a firm that sells on care and not on price.

## Convoi exceptionnel

Heavy haulage and abnormal loads: a transformer on a low-loader at night, a boat across three départements, a crane's ballast with two escort cars ahead of it. The visitor is a site manager or a buyer who has a load, a date and a permit to obtain, and wants to know whether this firm has moved the like. The photograph of a convoy lies behind the name across the opening, the licences and the authorisations read as ruled rows with their numbers, and what is carried runs down as rows, each with its limits. Indigo with the rust of a rivet, panels stitched twice, sturdy and plain-spoken. The rates are slabs on one board because every load is priced alone; the careers page sets the escort drivers' cards beside the depot; the quote stands beside its invitation; and the questions on permits sit in two columns.

## Navette des îles

A water taxi and ferry to the islands, booked from a phone on the quay or the night before: foot passengers, bicycles, a wedding party, a builder's van on the barge on Thursdays. The photograph of the boat leaving the harbour lies behind the one promise, crossings every hour, and what it carries are four large tiles, people, bikes, luggage, freight. Teal water and a coral sun, as the crossing looks in July. The fares are rows on a board, each with its price at the end; the questions on weather, dogs and the last boat back are all open; the seasonal deckhands and skippers are cards on a board; and the request for a charter is a long page with the form bare on it, under a header that centres the name over the pages like the sign on the ticket office.
