# florist

Twelve ways one florist is drawn: five as a studio where the flowers are the loud part, four as a shop with a counter, three as a stall in the sun. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is what the visitor is taken to have come for (a bouquet on the way home, the flowers of a wedding studied for a week, a wreath ordered the same morning, a bunch at the market's price), and so what the first screen leads with and how the bouquets are read: as a price list held from the top of its page, as tiles led by the bouquet of the day, or as rows with the price at the end.

## Maison de fleurs

The town florist most people mean by the word: a long first page that opens on one full-height photograph of the week's bouquet, then the three doors (the bouquets, the work, a day that matters), the week and a panorama of the streets round the shop. The bouquets are a price list from the top of their page, and the work and the occasions read like a magazine, a story broken once by its pictures. For an established house that sells a bouquet a day and a wedding a week, in black and white so that the only colour on the page is the flowers.

## Atelier de mariage

A florist who works by appointment and mostly for weddings: the home page is one cover and the name, with the hours and the door along its foot and nothing else to scroll to. The work is the page that sells, set as a story with every fifth picture twice the size; the request is an invitation on one side and its form on the other; the bouquets are a long quiet page, since a bride reads them once. For the atelier a couple studies for a week before writing, in aubergine and a little gold, the name centred over the pages like an invitation card.

## Fleurs de deuil

Funeral flowers, ordered by a family with two days and no wish to browse: no photograph on the opening at all, one sentence set large, then the week and the address as plain lines. The sprays and the wreaths are a price list from the top of their page; the request is a short account of how it goes (ordered by eleven, delivered to the chapel that afternoon) above a form with no panel round it, and the footer is three lines. For the florist beside the cemetery, or the one a funeral home sends people to. The cool blue-grey is only fair for a studio and right here: it says a service, never a celebration.

## Bouquets à offrir

The gift florist: most visitors are buying for somebody else, from a phone, and want to see what sixty francs looks like. The words sit centred over a wide photograph, the week lies across the page under the three doors, and the bouquets are tiles with the bouquet of the day twice the size of the rest. The work is a story for the few who read it, and the request one more long page. For a boutique that lives on birthdays, births and delivery the same day; pinned light, because the pinks and the soft corners are the whole register and would not survive a dark page.

## Clair-obscur

A designer florist whose work looks like a Dutch still life: the page is pinned dark, oxblood and gold, and the cover is one photograph of flowers lit from the side with the name low on it. The bouquets are a price list set like a wine list, the work a long page with every fifth picture large, and the request a story above a bare form. For the florist who dresses restaurants, hotels and evening receptions, whose clients choose on taste and would rather be shown three photographs than thirty.

## Comptoir du matin

The neighbourhood shop people look up from the pavement: the name beside the photograph on the first screen, today's hours and the map in the other half, and the bouquets from the top of their page with nothing to scroll past. The occasions page opens the same way and sets the invitation beside its form. For a florist most visitors ask two things of, whether it is open and what a bouquet costs, on warm paper and with nothing to wait for.

## Fleuriste du village

A family shop on a village square that sells as many plants and seedlings as cut flowers: deep green on paper, the name centred over the pages like a painted sign, the hours and the map as plain lines beside the opening, because people drive there and want to know when and where. Everything else is a long page, and the work is drawn with every fifth picture large: the church dressed for a wedding half the village went to. For the florist that has been there for three generations and sells to people who already know its name.

## Kiosque de la gare

A florist on the way to somewhere else: the first screen is the name beside one bouquet with three figures under a rule (what the bouquet of the day costs, the hour until which it is delivered), then the week across the page. The bouquets are rows read like a board of departures, the price at the end of each, from the top of their page; the work is one row of pictures to scroll past, and the footer three lines. For a kiosk or a corner shop that sells on price and speed to people who have eight minutes, in a yellow that can be seen from the platform.

## Enseigne de ville

A florist with several shops and a delivery van: a photograph bled to the window's edges with the words on it, the pages at the left of the bar and the action at the right as on a shop people order from, and a panorama of the streets under the week. The bouquets are a list from the top of their page led by the one that sells most, and the request is a long page for the office manager who orders the same flowers every Monday. For a group, or a florist that works for businesses, hotels and subscriptions: indigo and stitching say it is sturdy and that it will turn up.

## Étal du marché

A stall on the market square and no shop yet: glass panels on a wash of green and orange, pinned light because a stall is seen in the sun. The first screen is the stall on one side and when it stands on the other (the days, the square); the bouquets are a board of rows with the price at the right, and the work is one row of pictures. For a newcomer who sells bunches twice a week and takes weddings through one form, and wants to look like the season rather than like a boutique.

## Chez l’horticulteur

A grower who sells from the greenhouse door: the words centred over a wide photograph of the rows under glass, the three doors as a board, the week laid across the page and a panorama of the road in, since the nursery is at the end of a lane. The bouquets and the plants are rows led by one large tile, what is being cut this week. For a family nursery outside town that sells its own flowers and wants the green of the place on the page; people come by car on a Saturday, so the home page is the long one that tells them what they will find.

## Champ à cueillir

A field where people cut their own flowers and pay by the stem: no photograph on the opening, the name and one sentence set large on glass beside the days the field is open, yellow and black like the sign at the roadside. The prices are rows, a line each; the request (buckets of flowers for a wedding people arrange themselves) is an invitation beside its form, and the footer one centred column. For a self-service field or a cash-and-carry hall that sells on price and on the afternoon out.
