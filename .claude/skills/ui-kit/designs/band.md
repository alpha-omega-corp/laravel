# band

Fourteen ways one act is drawn, by who plays and who reads: the quintet that fills cellars every weekend and the dance band hired for weddings, the club's house ensemble and the act that started last year, the choir with a season and the village band a century old, the cabaret on tour, and, on white walls, the ensemble whose sleeves are half the work, the small label, the ensemble a programmer studies for a week, the turntable collective, the soloist in recital, the pocket opera on tour and the folk group with its linocut sleeves. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all fourteen; what changes is which audience the first screen answers, the one that comes to hear or the one that hires, and how the dates and the records are read: down a rule, as cards, as rows or as plates on a wall.

## Soir de concert

A quintet that plays cellars and clubs most weekends, looked up by people deciding where to go on Friday: the cover is the stage photographed with the name low on it and the next date in its note, and nothing else on the first screen. The dates run down a rule in the order a season is planned, the records page opens on the newest sleeve and goes straight to the list with the footer as its close, the band is told as a story with the road as one row of pictures that pauses it, and the request to hire stands beside its invitation right under the opening. Ember's oxblood and gold are the room by candlelight.

## Bal du samedi

A dance band that lives on weddings, company evenings and village fêtes, read by somebody with a date and a hall who is comparing three acts: the first screen sets the name and the one action beside the photograph of a full dance floor, then the doors to the dates, the records and the request. Who plays what is rows a host can read out to a caterer, the few public dates are cards because each is a chance to hear the band before signing, and the booking page goes from its opening straight to the form set beside its invitation. Sandstone's sienna keeps the warmth of a hall with the lights down, and the compact footer leaves the phone number as the last thing on every page.

## Cave à jazz

The house ensemble of a cellar club, followed by listeners who read liner notes: every page but the records is a magazine piece, the lead photograph of the cellar and then one narrow column, so the room, the season and the players are read rather than scanned. The records are rows, the words beside each sleeve, on a page that closes on the footer; the request to hire is written on the page like a letter, with no panel round it. Plum's aubergine and antique gold are a little theatrical, as a club is, and the name stands centred in the header and the footer like the sign over the stairs.

## Nuit électrique

An act that started last year and plays after midnight, found on a phone on the way to the club: the cover is one line of type on the glow, with no photograph owed, and the next night under it. Each date is a card to tap, the new record leads its page at twice the size of the rest, the nights are a mosaic where a crowd deserves the large picture, and the request to hire is a bare form under the opening. Nocturne's violet and magenta turn the dark into a night sky; the header parts the name from the links and the footer is one compact block, because nobody scrolls to an address at one in the morning.

## Saison du chœur

A choir or a chamber ensemble with a season, subscribers and a committee: the opening is centred like the title page of a programme, and under it the three doors to the season, the recordings and the singers. The season is read down a rule inside a narrow column, under the story of how it is planned; the recordings are rows, read more than looked at; the pictures are one row that pauses the page; and a parish or a festival that wants the ensemble writes to it at the end of a column that first explains how an engagement goes. Vellum's black, white and one red are as austere as concert dress.

## Fanfare du village

A brass band or a village music society a century old, whose site is read by the commune, by former members and by whoever organises this year's fête: home is the story, one photograph across the page and then the column, because a history is what this band has that a hired act has not. The year's outings run down a rule, the records and the scarf sold at the stand are led by the newest, the members are the direction's own portraits above the pictures of the last parade, and the long booking page sets the formulas out before the form beside its invitation and closes on the band's ask. Clay's terracotta and olive are a hall decorated by hand.

Blossom is only fair on this direction and chosen for exactly that: on the dark its pink and violet read as a neon sign.

Quill's bone paper, blue-black ink and brass are a bound catalogue, with the name parted from the pages in the header and a compact colophon at the foot.

## Veillée

A folk group that plays barn dances, trad festivals and the bal of every village fête, whose records come in sleeves the fiddler prints from linocuts: home is the long page under a photograph of a barn full of dancers, then the season down a rule, read in order from the first spring bal to the harvest festival. The records are rows, each with its sleeve, its tunes and its price, the players a list of who plays what, the road a row of pictures that scrolls, and an organiser writes at the end of a column that says what a bal needs: a floor, a caller, two hours. Clay's terracotta and olive on warm paper are the hand-printed sleeve and the hay, only fair on white walls and the right one for a band nobody would hang in a gallery.
