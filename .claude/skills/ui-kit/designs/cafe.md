# cafe

Twelve ways one café is drawn, by what kind of café it is and which hour it lives on: the neighbourhood counter looked up before leaving the house and the kiosk found on a phone at the water's edge, the lunch place that sells on its price and the roaster that sells on its craft, the tea-room of three generations and the coffee bar that opened last year, the village's one room and the coffee house that is a stage at night. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all twelve; what changes is which fact the first screen leads with (the hours, a photograph, the cup or a sentence), how the board is read (rows beside the hours, a narrow price list, cards) and whether the evenings are a board, a list of dates or a story.

## Café du coin

The neighbourhood café most people look up before leaving the house: the name beside this week's hours and the door on the first screen, the board in rows with its prices from the top of its page, and the week's evenings pinned on one board. For a café whose regulars ask two things, whether it is open yet and what the dish of the day costs, and whose hours are therefore drawn as plain lines in the side column rather than dressed up. Sandstone's printed paper keeps it warm and says nothing louder than the prices.

## Pause de midi

A café that lives between 11:30 and 14:00, between offices and a school, and sells on its price: the name beside the photograph with the dish of the day's figure under it, the week's hours as a strip straight after, and the streets around the door across the page, because half its visitors are coming for the first time on a lunch break. The board is a long carte with its sections side by side from the top of its page, and the evenings are only a list of dates down a rule for whoever stays in town. Marigold is loud and cheerful on purpose, and the footer is three lines: the close is the dark band above it.

## Salon de thé

The tea-room beside the pastry shop, kept by one family for three generations, where the busy hour is four in the afternoon: grandmothers, children after school, a slice of cake and a pot of tea. The name is centred over the photograph of the cake stand with the hours and the door beside it; the list is short enough for each cake and each pot to be a card worth looking at, and the few afternoons it holds (the Saint-Nicolas tea, the Advent songs) are cards too. Blossom is only fair on counter, since its borderless pink softens the ink rules the counter is drawn with, and that is the point here: a tea-room is cake boxes and ribbon, not a zinc. The name sits centred in the header and the footer as on the lid of the box.

## Buvette du lac

A kiosk café on the lakeshore, open when the weather is, found on a phone by somebody already standing on the quay in full sun: the first screen is words alone, set large, with the hours as plain lines and the map bare beside them, and no photograph to wait for on one bar of signal. The scheme is pinned light because the page is read outdoors at noon, and lagoon's teal and coral are the water and the parasols. The prices are in rows from the top of their page, the summer's evenings a list of dates read in order, and the footer is three lines under the band.

## Café du village

The one café of a village, which is also its meeting room: the card match, the brass band's lotto, the commune's aperitif. The terrace is photographed behind the name, the week's hours run across the page straight after it, and what is on is a board, each date a card, the way notices are pinned beside the door. For a place whose visitors are half villagers checking Thursday's lotto and half walkers deciding whether to stop, so the board of evenings matters as much as the carte, which is set in columns on a long page with the story of the house after it. Orchard's deep green and drawn borders look like a painted sign; the pages stand at the left of the header and the name in the middle.

## Bar à café

A specialty coffee bar that opened last year: eighteen seats, pale wood, one grinder per origin. The cover is one photograph of a cup and the name, with the hours and the door quiet at its foot and nothing else to scroll to; the prices are a narrow column with dotted leaders, read like a wine list; the tastings and the story of the house are set like a magazine, because the people who come already know what a filter coffee is and want to read where this one is from. For the newcomer that sells on craft. Vellum's black, white and one red keep the chrome out of the cups' way.

## Brûlerie

A roaster with a bar at the front of the roasting room, studied for a week by people who will then buy its beans every month: the name beside the photograph of the drum, three portraits leading to the coffees, the tastings and the house, and the streets around the door across the page for the first visit. Each coffee is a card with its origin and its price, each tasting a card with its date, and the story of the house runs long in one column because that is what this visitor reads before trusting a roaster. Clay's terracotta and olive are jute sacks and green beans; the pages stand at the left of the header and the name in the middle, like the label on a bag.

## Café-galerie

The café of a gallery or an art-house cinema, fullest after the doors close on the exhibition: the cover is the current show's photograph with the name centred over it, like its poster, and the season of openings and talks is told as a magazine piece with the dates down a rule. Nocturne is only fair on studio, its violet and magenta louder than the calm the direction wants, but pinned dark it becomes the night sky outside an opening, which is when this café is full. The price list stays one narrow column, the house gets a long page of its own, the name is centred in the header and the footer is three lines under the cover.

## Café-concert

A café that is a stage three nights a week: one photograph of the room with the band in it and the name low on it, tonight's hours as plain lines at its foot. The evenings are cards, each concert with its date, because the programme is what people open the site for; the carte is one quiet column for whoever wants to know what a glass costs, and the story of the room reads like a magazine. For a place people follow for its music rather than its coffee. Ember's oxblood and gold are the lamps over the tables, and the footer is three lines since the cover closes the page itself.

## Grand café

The old house on the square, open for a century, with mirrors, marble tables and waiters in aprons: the long page opens on the room photographed, then the week's hours across it, three doors and the square on a wide map. The carte is long, so its sections stand side by side like the printed card; the season's evenings are dates down a rule inside a magazine column, and the story of the house is told at length, since the house is what it sells. Vellum on the dark is black, white and one red, as its menus have always been printed, and the name is centred over the pages like a letterhead and again in the footer.

## Coin du feu

A country café with a stove, open from the afternoon, where walkers and the village end the day: the room on one side of the first screen, tonight's hours and the way to the door on the other, both drawn plain, because what a visitor checks is whether it is worth the road tonight. The evenings are a short list of dates read in order, the carte one column, and the house a long page of photographed doors. For a room that is the reason to come and a door that is hard to find. Sandstone's warm serif on the dark is firelight on old paper.

## Café littéraire

A café across the street from a bookshop that holds a reading every other Friday: the first screen is a sentence set large with no photograph, as a book opens on its first line, and the hours at its foot. The readings are told like a magazine piece in one narrow column, and the name is centred in the header and the footer as on a title page. For a quiet room whose regulars come for the evening's author rather than for the cup, and who read a page to its end. Plum's aubergine and antique gold are a little theatrical, as a reading is.
