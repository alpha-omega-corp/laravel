# construction

Fifteen ways one builder is drawn, eight on the yard and seven at the counter. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is who is expected to arrive (a commune with a tender, a family with a plot, an architect, a mason looking for a job) and so what the first screen leads with: the figures, the hoarding, the record, the phone or the price. The work is read as slabs on a board, as cards down a long page or as a ruled record, and the quote is either one field away or at the end of a road the visitor has been shown.

## Entreprise générale

The regional general contractor most briefs describe: a firm of fifty to a hundred that pours its own structures and answers both a family and a commune. The first screen sets the name beside a pour in progress with three figures under it, then the trades and the certifications as rows read by their number, since half the visitors are checking them against a tender. The work is a board of slabs led by the record itself, each job with its one figure, and the quote page opens beside the map of the yard because a client of this size comes to sign.

## Palissade

A town builder known by the hoardings round its sites: the home page is one cover, a photograph of a site bled to the edges with the name over it as large as on the fence, and everything else waits quietly below. For a firm whose clients have walked past its cranes for years and arrive already knowing the name; they want jobs like theirs, so the work runs down a long page with each photograph beside its facts, and the quote page puts the invitation on one side, the form on the other and the streets round the yard across the page.

## Génie civil

A civil works contractor that lives on public tenders: pipes, roads, retaining walls, a footbridge. Nobody here is sold by a photograph, so the opening is words alone set large over a board of slabs, the record is a ruled index read by place, year and metres, the licences are rows with their numbers, and the method is a timeline because its stages are unequal, a ten-day estimate and a fourteen-month site. Pinned dark on graphite: the engineer's office that reads it wants a document, not a brochure, and the hazard band is the only colour on the page.

## Le groupe

A group of several hundred with branches in three cantons, read by investors, general planners and graduates. The header is set as a group's is, the pages at the left and the name in the middle, the opening centres its words over one wide photograph, the trades are four or five large tiles rather than a list, and the certifications are a muted row of marks, because at this size they are assumed. Harbour's blue takes the site out of the yard and into the boardroom; the careers page puts the open positions as cards straight under the opening, with no band after them, since hiring is half of what a group's site is for.

## Vieilles pierres

A builder who mostly transforms what is already standing: farms of 1789, village houses, barns turned into flats. Its clients are owners who are afraid of what a wall hides, so the first screen puts the name beside the address and the trades follow as plain rows, the method is four or five equal steps in a row they can count, and the quote is a bare form on the page with the map plain beside the opening. Vellum is only fair on the yard, since the hazard band loses its bite on paper, and that is the reason it is here: old stone wants a quieter site, pinned light so the paper stays paper.

## On embauche

For the builder whose site is looked at on a phone in a site hut at lunch as often as by a client.

## Halles et hangars

An industrial builder that puts up halls, depots and farm buildings and sells on square metres and months. There is no photograph on the cover at all: the name and the promise set as large as a hoarding with three figures under them, then the trades as a handful of tiles and the certifications as one row of marks. The work is a board of slabs because every job here is sized by a single figure, and the method is a short row of equal steps, the way a hall is actually built. For a client who is a factory manager with a deadline, not a family with a dream.

Orchard's green says young and local rather than heavy; the quote page is the long one, the invitation beside the form and the streets round the workshop across the page, because a first client needs to be shown where they are.

## Villas clés en main

A builder of turnkey houses for private clients, who study the site for a week before they dare to write. The photograph is behind the words on every opening, a finished villa on its slope, the work runs down its page as a few large houses with their photograph beside the months and the price held, and the method is a timeline straight under its opening, with the footer as the close, because the road from permit to keys is what they are buying. Lagoon is only good at the counter, and it is chosen for what it is: water and light, for a firm that builds above a lake. The careers page is a plain list; nobody comes here for it.

## Atelier béton

A small firm that sells on craft: fair-faced concrete, polished floors, stairs cast in place, working for architects who send it the difficult part of a job. Pinned dark on ember, which is only fair at the counter and is here on purpose: the site should read as a workshop in the evening, not a yard at noon. The home page is a board, the work a short series of large rows each with its photograph, the method a timeline since a formwork takes weeks and a pour an hour, and the quote a bare form beside the plain map, for somebody who already knows what they want cast.

## Ossature bois

A timber-frame builder whose houses and extensions are cut in its own hall and stood up on site in a week. The first screen sets the name beside the hall with a wall panel on the trestles and three figures under it — square metres a year, days on site, tonnes of carbon kept — then the trades as tiles and the labels a client's bank asks for as rows. The work is a board of slabs, the method a short row of equal steps beside its opening because prefabrication is the argument, and the quote is the long page with the form beside its invitation and the hall on a wide map, since clients are invited to watch a house being made. Denim is good on the yard and cooler than its hazard band: an engineer's blue for a builder that sells precision rather than muscle.

## Isolation et façades

A renovation firm that insulates: façades, roofs, windows and heat pumps for owners of houses built in the seventies, most of them with a subsidy form in hand. The opening sets the promise beside a scaffolded façade, the labels and approvals that make the subsidy payable follow as one row of marks, and the jobs done are a ruled list from the top of their page, each with the kilowatt-hours it saved. The method is a timeline beside its opening because the subsidy has stages of its own — the audit, the application, the works, the payment — and the quote is a form beside its invitation with the map plain next to it. Harbour's cool blue-grey reads like an energy certificate, for owners who compare three estimates and a government table.

## Terre et paille

A small builder in natural materials: straw-bale walls, earth plaster, lime and timber, for families who chose the material before they chose the firm. They come from far, so the first screen is a photograph of a wall being plastered behind the name, over a board where the trades are a few tiles; the jobs done are a board of their own, each house with its one figure. The method is a row of equal steps on a long page, since a straw wall is built in the dry season and in that order, the careers page is a plain list of the apprenticeships it offers, and the quote is the long page that shows the way to the workshop on a wide map. Clay is good at the counter and is the material itself: terracotta and olive for walls made of earth.
