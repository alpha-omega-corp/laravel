# festival

Twelve ways one festival is drawn, across the three looks it may take: the pass sold from a clean front page, the night photographed, and the ground and the season up front. The pages and the prefabs are the same in all of them; what changes is which fact the first screen leads with — the dates, the price, the stage, the way up — and how the programme and the line-up are read: on a board, down a rule of dates, or as a piece of writing with the practical part under it.

## Rendez-vous d’août

The festival most people mean: a few thousand visitors, a presale, and a programme that fills in over the spring. The first screen is the promise and the dates centred over one action, the programme and the line-up are boards a visitor takes in at a glance, and the passes stand over the questions people ask before buying one. For an established open-air weekend whose site has two jobs, selling the pass and answering who is on when.

## Jour J

The three days themselves, when the site is the programme in a pocket, read on a phone in the sun at the gate. For a festival that rewrites its home page the week it opens; pinned light, in harbour's calm blue, so it reads outdoors at noon.

## Première édition

A festival that has never happened: there is no last year to show, so the people on the bill and the people behind it carry the site. The promise alone on the cover, the few dates of the programme as cards on a board, each guest as a row with a line about them, and the volunteer roles as cards that each get their paragraph, since a first edition recruits before it sells. For a new, small event that wants to look young and friendly rather than established.

## Marché de jour

A fair or a market weekend rather than a concert: stalls, tastings and a few demonstrations, in daylight, free or nearly. The home page opens on the ground and puts a wide map straight under it, because the visitor's first question is where to park; the programme and the exhibitors are boards to browse, and the tickets page is a long plain one where the price is a row and the questions are the content. Fresh's green is only fair on this look, but it says produce and morning, which is what this event sells.

## Trentième édition

A festival with a history: the same field for thirty summers, regulars who know the way and come for the story as much as for the bill. The first screen is a sentence rather than a photograph, the programme, the line-up and volunteering are each told as a piece of writing with the practical part under it, the guests are rows with something said about each, and the passes are plain rows on a long page. For an association prouder of who built it than of who headlines it.

## Pâturage

An open-air festival on a mountain pasture: one landscape with the dates over its horizon, and straight under it a wide map, because the first question is how to get up there. The programme is told day by day like a route, the dates down a rule under it; the passes stand over the questions about camping, the weather and the shuttle, every answer open. For an event where the place is the headliner and the weather is part of the programme.

## Fête de village

The village's own weekend: a harvest fair, a désalpe or a fête with a parade, a brass band and a raffle, run by the families who have always run it. A long home page that shows everything at once, the programme as cards because each of its few moments is an outing of its own, the people on the bill told as a story, and volunteering told as one too, since half the village is on the rota. Orchard's warm fruit colours say season and produce where alpine would say altitude.

The programme and the line-up are read as the story of three evenings by the water. Lagoon's blue-green is the lake, and it is pinned dark because the event starts at dusk.
